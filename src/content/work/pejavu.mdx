## The opening

Ryan runs Peja Vu solo — food truck by day, catering on weekends, private dinners in Sheboygan homes on request. His old Wix site was fine as a business card, but every menu change, every hours update, every catering inquiry meant a phone call and a manual edit. He wanted the site to earn its keep: take bookings, show current menu, capture leads, and let him update everything from a phone between services.

I came in through Doxa as the lead designer and frontend engineer on a bespoke Next.js + Payload rebuild. The scope wasn't just a nicer website — it was a small operational system disguised as one.

## Constraints that shaped the build

- **Chef-owned, not agency-managed.** Every content surface Ryan needs to touch had to be editable through a Payload UI that reads like a kitchen ticket, not a database. If Ryan can't find the "add a special" button in ten seconds, the system fails.
- **Sheboygan-specific trust signals.** Local booking behavior needs local proof — real Google reviews, real address (redacted from JSON-LD where personal), real Wisconsin phone, real photos of Ryan's food (not stock).
- **Booking, not just contact.** Weddings and corporate catering need a real quote flow with dates, headcount, service style, and dietary counts — not a `contact@` mailto.
- **Mobile-first, thumb-first.** Half the traffic comes from Instagram taps during lunchtime. Every CTA has to land under a thumb without pinch-zooming.

## How it's built

The stack settled on **Next.js 16 App Router** with three route groups: `(frontend)` for the public site, `(payload)` for the CMS UI, and `(staff)` for authenticated operator pages Ryan uses on his phone. **Payload v3** runs in the same Next process, backed by **Postgres** in production with a **SQLite fallback** for local dev — one binary, one deploy target.

**Radix UI** primitives power the accessible form + menu + dialog surfaces. **Motion** (formerly Framer Motion) handles the section reveals and the food-truck-on-a-road illustration without hurting Lighthouse. **Leaflet** renders the coverage map on the catering page. **Sonner** for toasts, **Lucide** for icons, **Sharp** for on-the-fly image optimization out of S3-compatible storage.

The **Kitchen Console** (the admin UI) is tuned specifically for Ryan: menu items have dietary tag pickers instead of freeform text, reviews have a moderation toggle that's the biggest button on the page, hours have a "close for the day" one-click, and the catering inbox surfaces new quote requests with a timeline of every touchpoint.

## Brand system

Chef-owned, warm, unfussy. The palette is a cream ground (`#FDF6EA` range) with burgundy accents (`#5A1F14` range) and a soft brown for tertiary text. The wordmark pairs a hand-drawn "Peja Vu" cursive with an all-caps sans classification strip ("FOOD · TRUCK · CATERING"). Every page carries the same three brand elements: the cream ground, the burgundy CTA color, and a hand-drawn food-truck illustration that anchors the home page and shows up in miniature across the footer and social exports.

The whole system is bound to Payload's global "brand theme" record, so any future rebrand touches one document, not fifty CSS files.

## What I learned

Building for a solo chef-owner is a totally different constraint than building for a marketing team. The value isn't a beautiful design system — it's whether Ryan opens the admin between services and edits the menu without calling. Every clever thing I could have done (nested collections, custom field types, complex relationships) got measured against: does this make Ryan's Tuesday afternoon easier or harder? Everything that made it harder got cut.
